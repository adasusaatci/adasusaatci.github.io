AERODYNAMICS

Lockheed Martin X-59 Quesst

There's nothing wrong with the aircraft, except that it's on fire.

NASA’s X-59, developed in collaboration with Skunk Works, Lockheed Martin’s advanced development division known for its highly classified projects, recently reached Mach 1.4 at an altitude of 55,000 feet. Of course, many aircraft are capable of reaching speeds around Mach 1.4. What makes the X-59 different, however, is that its goal is not simply to fly fast. It is designed to achieve supersonic flight without producing the disruptive **sonic boom** normally associated with aircraft exceeding the speed of sound.

The more closely I looked at the aircraft, the more familiar its design seemed. Eventually, I realized that I had seen something remarkably similar years ago in John D. Anderson’s *Fundamentals of Aerodynamics*. Until I figured out where I remembered it from, I genuinely spent some time believing that I had somehow been designing aircraft in my dreams. Unfortunately, that turned out not to be the case. Since I couldn’t find a single comprehensive source explaining the engineering behind this design—despite the concept itself appearing in textbooks—I decided to put together a few notes, primarily as a reference for myself. I’m sharing them here in case anyone else finds them useful. Needless to say, none of the information, photographs, or analyses presented here are originally mine. I’ll try to cite the relevant sources throughout the article.

What Exactly is X-59?

The X-59 is a purely experimental aircraft designed to investigate how the shock waves generated during supersonic flight can be shaped and controlled to reduce the resulting sonic boom. Humans have had the ability to achieve supersonic flight for nearly 80 years. Historically, however, supersonic capability has primarily been associated with military aircraft, particularly fighter jets. But has supersonic passenger travel ever been attempted? It certainly has—and successfully. The most famous example is Concorde, developed jointly by Britain and France. Concorde made its first flight in 1969 and remained in commercial service until 2003. Today, surviving Concorde aircraft are preserved and displayed in museums around the world.

The main factor behind Concorde’s retirement was its poor economic sustainability. At supersonic speeds, wave drag becomes a major component of the aircraft’s total drag, contributing to the high thrust—and therefore high fuel consumption—required for sustained supersonic flight. This resulted in very high operating costs per passenger. The second issue brings us to the main subject of this article: the phenomenon known as the sonic boom . I will explain what a sonic boom is and how it is generated in a separate section, but in simple terms, it is the technical name for the explosion-like sound heard on the ground when an aircraft flies overhead at supersonic speed. These pressure disturbances can still be clearly perceived on the ground even when the aircraft is flying at high altitude. Apart from being disruptive to people and communities, sufficiently strong sonic booms can also affect structures and, under certain conditions, have been associated with damage such as broken windows. For this reason, routine civilian supersonic flight over land was heavily restricted in many countries. This significantly limited Concorde’s potential operating routes, since its defining advantage—supersonic passenger travel—could primarily be exploited over the ocean. The situation became even more difficult following the crash of Air France Flight 4590 in 2000. Although Concorde returned to commercial service after safety modifications were introduced, the accident further affected demand and the economics of operating the aircraft. Combined with already high operating and maintenance costs and declining passenger demand, these factors ultimately led to Concorde’s retirement in 2003.

However, this should not be misunderstood: the X-59 was not designed as a commercial aircraft. Its purpose is experimental. NASA had already been conducting theoretical and experimental research into low-boom aerodynamics long before the X-59 reached the preliminary design stage in 2016. In fact, one of the most important predecessors of the X-59 was the Shaped Sonic Boom Demonstration (SSBD) program in the early 2000s. As part of the program, a Northrop F-5E Tiger II was extensively modified, particularly around its nose and forward fuselage, to investigate whether an aircraft’s geometry could be deliberately shaped to alter its sonic-boom pressure signature. Flight tests conducted in 2003 demonstrated that aircraft shaping could indeed modify the shock-wave pattern and reduce the intensity of the resulting sonic boom measured on the ground. These experiments provided important real-world evidence for concepts that would later contribute to the development of low-boom aircraft such as the X-59.

To reduce the intensity of the shock waves reaching the ground—and therefore the magnitude of the sudden pressure change—a structure known as a nose glove was added to the nose of the Northrop F-5E. The modified nose had a distinctive geometry: its upper surface was relatively flat, while the lower surface featured a pronounced bulge. Engineers predicted that this geometry would alter the formation and propagation of the shock waves around the aircraft, preventing them from simply coalescing into a stronger shock as they travelled toward the ground. Subsequent wind-tunnel experiments and flight tests validated the concept. Measurements showed that deliberate aircraft shaping could modify the resulting ground-level pressure signature and reduce the intensity of the sonic boom. However, the project itself was not developed into an operational aircraft program.

Sonic Boom

Before taking a closer look at the design of the X-59, it is worth briefly discussing what a sonic boom actually is and why it occurs. A sonic boom is an acoustic phenomenon associated with the shock waves generated by an aircraft during supersonic flight. When these shock waves reach the ground, they produce rapid changes in pressure that are perceived by the human ear as a characteristic boom. The formation of shock waves is an inherent consequence of supersonic flight and cannot simply be eliminated. The objective of low-boom aircraft design, therefore, is not to eliminate shock waves altogether, but to control their formation and propagation in order to reduce the magnitude and rate of the pressure changes that reach the ground—and consequently reduce the perceived loudness of the sonic boom.

A simple axisymmetric body can generate relatively simple shock structures. A real aircraft, however, is considerably more complex. Its nose, canopy, wings, engine inlets, control surfaces, and other geometric features can each generate their own compression and expansion waves. As these disturbances propagate through the atmosphere, they interact and may merge with one another. Far from a conventional supersonic aircraft, this process can produce the characteristic N-wave pressure signature, consisting primarily of a rapid pressure rise followed by a gradual pressure decrease and another rapid change toward ambient pressure. These abrupt pressure changes are what produce the familiar double-boom often associated with supersonic aircraft.

But why do shock waves form in the first place? Any object moving through the atmosphere disturbs the air around it. During subsonic flight, these pressure disturbances propagate through the surrounding air at approximately the local speed of sound. Since the aircraft itself is travelling slower than these disturbances, the air ahead of it can effectively “respond” to its approach: pressure information propagates upstream and the flow adjusts relatively smoothly around the aircraft. The situation changes once the aircraft exceeds the local speed of sound. The aircraft is now travelling faster than pressure disturbances can propagate upstream through the surrounding air. The flow ahead of the aircraft can no longer continuously adjust to its approach. Instead, compression disturbances accumulate and coalesce into extremely thin regions across which pressure, density, temperature, and flow velocity change abruptly. These regions are what we call shock waves.
