FLIGHT CONTROL SYSTEMS

Qantas Flight 72

For everyone who asks me why I’m afraid of flying...

On October 7, 2008, an Airbus A330-303 departed from Singapore Changi Airport for Perth, Australia. During the flight, a serious in-flight control event forced the aircraft to make an emergency landing at Learmonth Airport in Western Australia. None of the 303 passengers or 12 crew members on board were killed; however, many people were injured as a result of the incident.

What Happened?

Qantas Flight 72 was cruising normally at FL370 (37,000 ft) when one of the aircraft’s three Air Data Inertial Reference Units (ADIRUs), ADIRU 1, began sending intermittent erroneous data to the aircraft’s systems. Shortly afterward, the autopilot disconnected, and the pilots were presented with numerous warning and caution messages, many of which indicated faults that did not actually exist.

The pilots re-engaged Autopilot 2; however, it disconnected again shortly afterward. Approximately one minute later, the aircraft experienced a sudden, uncommanded nose-down pitch. The pitch angle reached approximately −8.4°, and the aircraft lost about 650 ft of altitude.

During the sudden pitch-down maneuver, the aircraft experienced a normal acceleration of approximately −0.8 g. As a result, particularly unrestrained passengers and crew members were thrown toward the cabin ceiling, with some sustaining serious injuries after striking the ceiling and overhead baggage compartments.

After the pilots regained control of the aircraft, it was brought back to approximately FL370. However, a few minutes later, a second, smaller uncommanded pitch-down event occurred. The crew continued the flight under manual control, decided to divert to Learmonth Airport, and landed the aircraft safely.

There were no fatalities. However, at least 110 of the 303 passengers and 9 of the 12 crew members were injured, with 12 people sustaining injuries classified as serious.

One of the most notable features of the A330 is its fly-by-wire flight control system, which has become a defining characteristic of Airbus aircraft. Although fly-by-wire technology is now widely used in modern commercial aircraft, Airbus remains one of the manufacturers most closely associated with the concept.

A First Look at the Accident

During cruise flight, an aircraft suddenly pitching nose-down without any pilot input might initially suggest that the flight control system was attempting to protect the aircraft from a potentially hazardous flight condition. For example, if the angle of attack reaches a critical level, Airbus’s fly-by-wire system can limit pilot control inputs to prevent the aircraft from entering the stall region and attempt to keep the angle of attack within a safe range.

However, in the case of Qantas Flight 72, we know that the aircraft was not actually approaching a stall. Furthermore, the autopilot was not engaged when the first sudden pitch-down event occurred.

This raises an important question: if the pilots did not command the nose-down movement and the autopilot was disengaged, what commanded the elevators to move the aircraft nose-down?

To answer this question, we first need to examine how the A330 collects flight data from the outside environment and how that information reaches the aircraft’s flight control computers.

Understanding the Airbus A330

The A330 is Airbus’s family of long-range, twin-engine, wide-body passenger aircraft. First flown in 1992, the A330 was developed as a continuation of Airbus’s earlier wide-body aircraft programs, drawing particularly on experience gained from the A300 and A310.

The A330 uses the digital fly-by-wire flight control philosophy introduced into service by Airbus with the A320 family. In terms of cockpit layout and flight control systems, it continues Airbus’s common design philosophy across its aircraft families.

The original A330 family was offered with three different engine options: the General Electric CF6-80E1, Pratt & Whitney PW4000, and Rolls-Royce Trent 700. The later A330neo family, however, is powered exclusively by Rolls-Royce Trent 7000 engines.

Today, the A330 family remains in production through the A330neo generation, which consists of the A330-800 and A330-900 variants.

A330 Aircraft Sensors

Air Data Sensors

Pitot Tube

The pitot tube is one of the fundamental air-data sensors used on a wide range of aircraft, from small general aviation aircraft to large commercial airliners. Contrary to a common simplification, however, a pitot tube does not directly measure airspeed. Instead, it senses the total pressure, also known as stagnation pressure, of the airflow.

Total pressure alone is not sufficient to determine airspeed. The static pressure, sensed through static ports located on the aircraft, is also required. The difference between total pressure Pt and static pressure Ps corresponds to dynamic pressure q.

\[ q = P_t - P_s \]

The A330 is equipped with three independent pitot probes. They are located on the forward fuselage at positions designed to provide reliable pressure measurements from the external airflow.

Another important point is that the pitot probe does not perform the airspeed calculation itself. Its function is to sense pressure. The resulting pressure information is then transmitted through the aircraft's air data system to the Air Data Inertial Reference Units (ADIRUs), where it is used in the computation of air data parameters such as airspeed.

When airflow enters the forward-facing opening of the pitot probe, it is decelerated until it reaches a stagnation point. As the flow velocity approaches zero, its pressure increases, allowing the pitot probe to sense the total pressure, also known as stagnation pressure.

Under the assumption of low-speed, incompressible flow, total pressure can be expressed as the sum of static and dynamic pressure. Therefore, dynamic pressure can be obtained from the difference between total pressure Pt and static pressure Ps.

Dynamic pressure is directly related to airspeed. However, for aircraft such as the A330 operating at high subsonic speeds, the compressibility of air can no longer be neglected. Therefore, the aircraft's air data system uses compressible-flow relationships when determining airspeed from pressure measurements.

Because pitot probes are directly exposed to the external environment, they can be affected by icing, water, foreign objects, or other contaminants. Incorrect pressure data from the pitot system can result in erroneous airspeed information, potentially creating serious flight-safety hazards.

For this reason, pitot probes are equipped with electrical heating systems to prevent ice formation during flight. When the aircraft is parked on the ground for extended periods, pitot covers may also be installed to protect the probe openings from foreign objects and contaminants. These covers must be removed before flight.

In aircraft, particularly commercial airliners, it is essential that critical flight information does not depend on a single sensor. For this reason, air data systems use multiple sensors capable of measuring the same physical quantity, providing redundancy in the event of a sensor failure or unreliable measurement. The Airbus A330 is therefore equipped with three pitot probes, which form part of the aircraft's redundant air data architecture.

Static Ports

In addition to total pressure, static pressure is required to determine airspeed. Static ports are positioned on the aircraft fuselage in locations designed to minimize the influence of the dynamic pressure associated with the aircraft's motion through the air, allowing them to sense the ambient static pressure of the surrounding atmosphere.

Under the basic assumption of incompressible flow, the difference between the total pressure Pt obtained from the pitot probe and the static pressure Ps obtained from the static ports corresponds to the dynamic pressure q. These pressure measurements are then processed by the aircraft's air data system and used to determine airspeed and other air data parameters.

\[ V = \sqrt{\frac{2(P_t - P_s)}{\rho}} \]

In addition to the three pitot probes, the A330 is equipped with a total of six static ports distributed on both sides of the fuselage. Together with the three pitot probes, these static pressure sources form part of the aircraft's redundant air data architecture, which provides pressure information to three independent air data channels.

Air Data Module (ADM)

The pneumatic pressure information obtained from the pitot probes and static ports cannot be used directly by the aircraft's digital avionics systems. Therefore, the pressure inputs are routed to units known as Air Data Modules (ADMs).

Inside each ADM, a pressure transducer converts the applied pneumatic pressure into an electrical signal. This signal is then processed and converted into digital pressure data, which is transmitted to the Air Data Inertial Reference Units (ADIRUs). In this way, the physical pressure measurements obtained from the pitot and static systems are converted into a form that can be processed by the aircraft's avionics systems.

The A330 is equipped with a total of eight Air Data Modules.

Angle of Attack Sensors

The A330 is equipped with vane-type Angle of Attack (AoA) sensors located on the forward fuselage and directly exposed to the external airflow. Each freely rotating vane aligns itself with the local airflow at its position, allowing the sensor to determine the local airflow angle relative to the aircraft.

The angular position of the vane is converted into an electrical signal within the sensor. This signal can then be used by the aircraft's air data system to determine the aircraft's angle of attack.

However, the angle measured directly by the sensor is not necessarily identical to the aircraft's actual angle of attack. This is because the sensor does not measure the undisturbed free-stream airflow, but rather the local airflow at its position on the fuselage.

The flow field around the aircraft's forward fuselage, sideslip, and different flight conditions can influence the local flow angle sensed by the vane. Therefore, the raw angular measurement from the sensor is not used directly as the aircraft's angle of attack.

The local flow-angle information measured by the sensor is transmitted to the ADIRU. The Air Data Reference (ADR) function of the ADIRU processes this information and applies the necessary corrections to produce the Angle of Attack (AoA) data used by the aircraft's systems.

Total Air Temperature (TAT) Sensors

Total Air Temperature (TAT) sensors are used to sense the total temperature of the airflow outside the aircraft. As air enters the probe, it is decelerated and its temperature is sensed by temperature-sensing elements within the probe. The sensor contains two separate sensing elements for redundancy.

The measured Total Air Temperature is not the same as the actual static temperature of the surrounding atmosphere, known as Static Air Temperature (SAT). As the airflow is decelerated within the probe, part of its kinetic energy appears as an increase in temperature. Consequently, particularly at high airspeeds, TAT is higher than SAT.

The electrical temperature information from the TAT sensor is transmitted to the Air Data Reference (ADR) function of the ADIRU. The ADR uses this information together with pressure data from the pitot and static systems to calculate SAT, Mach number, True Airspeed (TAS), and other air data parameters.

Static air temperature and static pressure can also be used to determine atmospheric air density.

\[ \rho = \frac{P_s}{R T_s} \]
\[ q = \frac{1}{2} \rho V_{TAS}^{2} \]

Inertial Sensors

Air Data Inertial Reference Unit (ADIRU)

Put simply, the Air Data Inertial Reference Unit (ADIRU) is one of the aircraft's critical avionics units, continuously providing aircraft systems with the information needed to answer three fundamental questions: “Where am I?”, “How am I moving?”, and “How am I moving relative to the surrounding air?”

The ADIRU combines two primary functions within a single unit: the Air Data Reference (ADR) and the Inertial Reference (IR).

The ADR receives digitized pitot and static pressure data from the ADMs, angular information from the Angle of Attack sensors, and Total Air Temperature (TAT) data from the temperature sensors. By processing these inputs, the ADR computes air data parameters such as Calibrated Airspeed (CAS), True Airspeed (TAS), Mach number, pressure altitude, and Angle of Attack (AoA).

The IR has a fundamentally different function from the ADR. To sense motion along and about the three aircraft axes, the system contains three accelerometers and three Ring Laser Gyros (RLGs). The accelerometers measure specific force components along three orthogonal axes, while the Ring Laser Gyros measure angular rates about these axes.

By processing and integrating these measurements, the IR determines navigation and inertial parameters such as aircraft attitude, heading, velocity, and inertial position. In simple terms, while the ADR determines how the aircraft is moving relative to the surrounding atmosphere, the IR provides information about the aircraft's motion, orientation, and position in space.

The information generated by the ADIRUs is distributed to numerous aircraft systems, including the flight control computers, flight displays, and other avionics systems. For redundancy, the A330 is equipped with three independent ADIRUs.

Of course, an aircraft contains many other types of sensors, including flight control surface position sensors, pilot control input sensors, landing gear sensors, and engine sensors. However, since these sensors are not directly required to understand the sequence of events that led to the Qantas Flight 72 incident, they will not be discussed in detail here.

A330 Sensor Architecture

So far, we have discussed several sensors that operate together as part of the aircraft's air data and inertial reference system. However, understanding the individual function of each sensor is only part of the picture. It is equally important to understand how the information produced by these sensors is combined, processed, and distributed to the aircraft's various systems.

Put simply, the ADIRU processes information received from the aircraft's air data sensors together with measurements from its internal accelerometers and gyros. It then generates flight and navigation information that can be used by the flight control computers, cockpit displays, and other avionics systems.

The A330 is equipped with three separate units that perform this function: ADIRU 1, ADIRU 2, and ADIRU 3.

In the normal configuration, information from ADIRU 1 is used on the captain's Primary Flight Display (PFD), while information from ADIRU 2 is used on the first officer's PFD.

ADIRU 3 primarily serves as an alternate source of air data and inertial reference information. If a problem occurs with ADIRU 1 or ADIRU 2, the pilots can use the cockpit switching system to select ADIRU 3 as an alternate source for the affected side.

Therefore, the three ADIRUs on the A330 should not be thought of simply as three independent systems supplying three separate displays. The information generated by the ADIRUs is used not only by the pilots' displays, but also by the flight control computers and numerous other avionics systems throughout the aircraft.

Data generated by ADIRU 1, ADIRU 2, and ADIRU 3 are continuously monitored and processed by the Flight Control Primary Computers (FCPCs). To prevent erroneous information from a single ADIRU from adversely affecting aircraft control, the flight control system uses various comparison, voting, monitoring, and fault-detection mechanisms.

One method that can be used when comparing redundant data sources is median voting. In this approach, three values representing the same parameter are compared, and the middle value is selected as the median reference.

For example, suppose the three ADIRUs provide airspeed values of 250 kt, 251 kt, and 310 kt. When the values are arranged in ascending order, 251 kt is the median value. The system can then compare the individual data sources with this reference to evaluate their consistency.

If the value from one source deviates from the reference by more than a predefined threshold, the system may also evaluate whether the discrepancy persists for a specified confirmation period. Depending on the parameter, monitoring logic, and fault criteria involved, data from the affected ADR or IR source may subsequently be rejected or no longer used by the relevant flight control function.

Median-based comparison is particularly effective in a single-failure scenario in which two sources provide similar values while the third produces a significantly different value.

However, if all three sources provide substantially different values, the fact that one value happens to be the mathematical median does not mean that it represents the physically correct value. For example, suppose the actual airspeed is 300 kt while the three sources provide values of 200 kt, 250 kt, and 300 kt. The mathematical median would be 250 kt, even though the physically correct value in this hypothetical example is 300 kt.

Therefore, redundancy and voting algorithms can significantly improve the fault tolerance of a system, but they do not guarantee that the true physical value can be identified under every possible combination of failures. In other words, having three computers does not mean that nature operates by democracy. Consider this a public service announcement.

What Happened During Qantas Flight 72?

Approximately four hours into the flight, while Qantas Flight 72 was cruising at FL370, ADIRU 1 began producing transient erroneous values, or data spikes, across multiple flight parameters. Shortly afterward, the autopilot disconnected and the pilots were presented with numerous warning and caution messages.

Approximately two minutes later, despite there being no nose-down command from the pilots and with the autopilot disengaged, the aircraft suddenly began an uncommanded pitch-down maneuver.

The nose-down command was not generated by the autopilot, but by the aircraft's Flight Control Primary Computers (FCPCs). Some of the erroneous and abnormally high Angle of Attack (AoA) values originating from ADIRU 1 were accepted by the flight control system and used in its control-law calculations. As a result, the FCPCs commanded a nose-down elevator response.

But how could erroneous information from ADIRU 1 affect the aircraft's control surfaces when the A330 was equipped with three independent ADIRUs?

Investigations conducted after the landing found no fault in any of the aircraft's three Angle of Attack sensors that could explain the event. ADIRU 1, however, was found to have generated numerous transient erroneous values across multiple flight parameters during the flight. ADIRU 2 and ADIRU 3 continued to operate normally.

The investigation determined that ADIRU 1 had experienced an extremely rare data-spike failure mode. Although the precise physical mechanism responsible for the failure could not be conclusively identified, the ATSB considered it likely that the CPU module within ADIRU 1 had, on some occasions, combined the data value of one flight parameter with the data label of another parameter.

However, erroneous data from ADIRU 1 alone should not have been sufficient to produce such an event. The A330 flight control system incorporated multiple layers of redundancy, consistency checking, and filtering intended to prevent erroneous information from a single ADIRU from adversely affecting aircraft control.

This is where the second part of the problem emerged.

The 1.2-Second Problem

The way the FCPCs processed Angle of Attack data differed from the processing of some other ADIRU parameters. Under normal conditions, when the AoA values from all three ADIRUs were valid and consistent with one another, the FCPCs used the average of AOA 1 and AOA 2 for their flight control calculations.

For example, consider the following Angle of Attack values from the three ADIRUs:

\[ AOA_1 = 5.0^\circ \] \[ AOA_2 = 5.2^\circ \] \[ AOA_3 = 5.1^\circ \]

Since all three values are consistent with one another, the FCPCs would continue using the AoA data normally.

Now suppose that AOA 1 suddenly increases to 50°:

\[ AOA_1 = 50^\circ \] \[ AOA_2 = 5.2^\circ \] \[ AOA_3 = 5.1^\circ \]

The FCPCs would detect that AOA 1 had deviated significantly from the other two sources. Instead of immediately using the abnormal value, the AoA processing logic could temporarily use a previously memorised valid value for AOA 1. This memorisation period lasted approximately 1.2 seconds.

For an isolated data spike, this logic provided an effective means of preventing a transient erroneous AoA value from entering the flight control calculations. Qantas Flight 72, however, presented an extremely unusual scenario. ADIRU 1 generated multiple erroneous AoA spikes, some of which occurred at intervals of approximately 1.2 seconds.

This highly specific timing exposed a previously unidentified limitation in the FCPCs' AoA processing logic. Although the algorithm was capable of detecting and filtering isolated erroneous values from a single ADIRU, the sequence of repeated spikes interacted with the 1.2-second memorisation logic in a way that had not been anticipated in the system design.

As a result, some of the erroneous high-AoA values originating from ADIRU 1 were able to pass through the filtering logic and enter the flight control calculations.

The FCPCs responded to these erroneous AoA values by generating nose-down elevator commands, resulting in the first sudden uncommanded pitch-down event experienced by Qantas Flight 72.

As a result, some of the erroneous high-AoA values originating from ADIRU 1 entered the FCPC flight control calculations. In response to these erroneous inputs, the FCPCs generated nose-down elevator commands, causing the aircraft to experience two sudden uncommanded pitch-down events.

The autopilot had already disconnected before the first pitch-down event occurred. The flight crew regained control of the aircraft manually, decided to divert to Learmonth Airport, and landed safely.

Following the incident, Airbus initially issued new flight crew procedures to be followed in the event of a similar ADIRU failure. The AoA processing logic within the A330/A340 Flight Control Primary Computer software was subsequently redesigned. The 1.2-second memorisation mechanism was removed, and additional monitoring was introduced to assess the consistency of the three AoA sources. A new mechanism was also implemented so that, once an ADR producing erroneous AoA data was identified, data from that ADR would be rejected for the remainder of the flight.

The ADIRU manufacturer also introduced modifications to the LTN-101 ADIRU to improve the system's ability to detect similar data transmission errors.

Qantas Flight 72 provides a striking example of how highly reliable systems can still behave unexpectedly when exposed to a very specific combination of conditions that was not anticipated during system design. At the time of the investigation, Qantas Flight 72 was the only known occurrence in which this FCPC design limitation had affected an aircraft's flight path, despite the A330/A340 fleet having accumulated more than 28 million flight hours.

Lessons Learned

Sometimes, finding a bug in an algorithm that has operated successfully for millions of flight hours requires not only the right input, but the right input arriving at exactly the wrong time.

At this point, all we can do is wish software engineers patience and mental clarity. And, unless absolutely necessary, perhaps stay home.
